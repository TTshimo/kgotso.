Understanding

What it actually feels like

Textbooks describe symptoms. This describes the experience, what it's like to live inside these conditions.

A note before you read This page describes depression, anxiety, trauma, and childhood neglect in plain, direct language, including a brief mention of suicidal thoughts. If any of that feels like too much right now, it's okay to close this and come back another time.
Before you read on None of this replaces a proper diagnosis. Think of it as a mirror that might help you recognise something in yourself or someone you love, and a nudge toward the "Get Help" page if it does.

The conditions

Depression

Not sadness you can shake off with a good day. It's waking up tired from sleep you did get. It's food tasting like nothing. It's watching yourself stop replying to messages and not having the energy to care that you did. Some days are fine. Then, for no clear reason, the weight comes back. It can last weeks, months, or even years.

Anxiety

A running commentary that won't turn off. Your body reacts to a text message the way it would to a threat; tight chest, racing thoughts, the urge to check something one more time. It's exhausting in a way that's hard to explain to someone who's never felt their own heartbeat in their throat over nothing in particular.

PTSD & trauma responses

A smell, a tone of voice, a certain kind of silence in a house and suddenly you're not in the present anymore. Your body remembers things your mind has tried to file away. Hypervigilance, flashbacks, and a nervous system stuck in "on guard" long after the danger has passed.

Bipolar disorder

Not "moody." Genuine swings between depressive lows and manic or hypomanic highs, periods of racing thoughts, reduced need for sleep, and impulsivity, followed by crashes that can feel devastating. The unpredictability is often as hard to live with as either extreme.

Burnout

What happens when "pushing through" stops working. Cynicism about things you used to care about, a sense that nothing you do makes a difference, and a tiredness that sleep doesn't fix. Often mistaken for laziness, by other people, and by the person going through it.

Reactive Attachment Disorder (RAD)

What can take root when a baby or young child doesn't get consistent comfort, warmth, or care from the adults around them, often through neglect, frequent changes in caregiver, or a home where love felt unpredictable. A child learns, very early, that reaching out doesn't reliably get met, so they stop reaching out. In adulthood it can look like a deep discomfort with closeness, a reflex to withdraw the moment a relationship gets real, or a numbness where affection should be. People who live with it are often mistaken for being cold or guarded, when really they never got to learn that connection is safe.

Complex PTSD (C-PTSD)

Different from single-incident PTSD, this comes from prolonged or repeated trauma, often in childhood, where there was no real way to escape. Alongside flashbacks and hypervigilance, it often carries a persistent sense of shame, difficulty regulating emotion, and a shaky sense of who you even are outside of survival mode.

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Not "liking things tidy." Intrusive, unwanted thoughts that loop and loop, paired with compulsions, mental or physical, done to try to quiet them. The relief never lasts, so the cycle repeats. It's exhausting, often secret, and rarely looks the way it's portrayed on screen.

Panic disorder

Sudden, intense episodes of fear that hit for no obvious reason, racing heart, shortness of breath, a feeling of losing control or even dying, peaking within minutes. Many people end up in an emergency room the first time it happens, certain something is physically wrong. Living with it often means organising your life around the fear of the next one.

Postpartum & perinatal mental health

The stretch of low mood after having a baby that goes beyond ordinary tiredness or the "baby blues." It can bring guilt, disconnection from the baby, intrusive worry, or a sense of failing at something everyone expects to feel natural. It's common, it's not a reflection of how much someone loves their child, and it's treatable.

When to take it seriously

  • It's lasted more than two weeks and isn't tied to one specific event
  • It's affecting your sleep, appetite, work, or relationships
  • You've started avoiding people or things you used to enjoy
  • You've had thoughts of not wanting to be here, see the suicide awareness page

Next step

If any of this sounds familiar, you don't have to figure out what it is on your own. The Get Help page lists real places you can reach out to.
